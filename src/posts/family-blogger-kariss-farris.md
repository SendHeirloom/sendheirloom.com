For photographer, videographer, and family blogger Kariss Farris, having grown up “in a bubble”  in Dallas hasn’t been all bad, especially since the pandemic started. “Almost everyone who I grew up with, my mom, my brothers, my aunts, my uncles—they all still live here,” she says. “People live across different parts of the area, but no more than 20 minutes away. We’re all very, very close, so keeping in touch has been pretty simple.” Farris shares her lovely family with the world through her [blog](http://www.karissfarris.com/blog), where she documents her three ridiculously cute kids, her faith, and the joyful day-to-day franticness of being a mom in 2021. Heirloom caught up with her for a chat about making memories and sharing traditions.

Where did you grow up?

I grew up in Dallas, Texas, and lived there until I went to college. I was an only child until I was nine, and then my mom had my three younger brothers. We're a blended family—my dad brought in my sister, who’s four years younger than me. So I was kind of an only child at first, and then ended up the oldest kid in a decent-sized family.

 

What were some of your favorite family traditions when you were a kid?

Christmas was the holiday for us—it still is, really. There were so many traditions leading up to it, and even after it: About two weeks beforehand, there was always a day where my grandmother would take all the women in our family to Neiman Marcus for lunch, then shopping and to see a movie afterward. The men knew that the girls were gone, and they needed to watch the kids—that was non-negotiable. A week before Christmas, my immediate family would drive to see the lights around town. On actual Christmas Day, we would all go to my grandmother's house. Then about two days later, our entire family would go to a lake house to hang out in the week before New Year's. Christmas is our year’s big event.

Were there other everyday traditions that you loved?

One was that my mom would take me to Barnes and Noble, get me a hot chocolate, and we would sit there and read together. That really gave me a love for reading, because she made it so fun—she made it an event. It was wonderful quality time just for the two of us. I want to do the same with my daughters.

 

Now that you’re a mom, what are some traditions you have with your kids?

Me and my oldest daughter have a lot of fun dancing together. I'm not the best dancer but I really don't care, and she doesn't know yet that she's not the best dancer, so it's great. Sometimes she'll get home from school and we'll have a dance party outside with the music blasting from the car. It’s a fun way to get our energy out and to bond.

What inspired you to start documenting your life online?

My first inspiration was the birth of my daughter: I wanted to share her with my friends and family, because she was this little Michelin Man bundle of rolls of cuteness. As I shared her, brands started sending me clothes and different things for her. So it kind of started organically and not on purpose—all I wanted to do was document my life as a mom.

 

When you were a kid, did your family take lots of home movies?

Oh yes. We have lots of home movies that were taken with this massive video camera you had to hold on your shoulder. The clips of my uncle Jonathan are the most hysterical. We’re 10 years apart, so he's more of an older brother than an uncle. Whenever someone was taping at home, no matter what they were filming, he would pop out of absolutely nowhere and go, "The camera should be on me." This little 10-year-old talking like Frank Sinatra, jumping in front of the camera.

 

I’m sure you have a giant treasure trove of videos of your kids on your phone—are there any you find yourself replaying repeatedly?

I do have countless videos on my phone—my phone is actually malfunctioning this week because of the amount of content on there. But my favorite would have to be of my daughter Ellie as a little girl. She has such a strong personality, and I love the videos we have of her at two years old, being defiant. For a while, we were trying to teach her the sign language for “please” and she could do it, but she only used it when she wanted to. There's a video where I was trying to give her a cookie but get her to say please, and she would not do it. I would do the action and she would go, “unh uh,” until she was pouting on the floor, refusing. I didn’t want to encourage the bad behavior, but it was so hilarious. And it brings me joy, especially now that we're out of that stage.

Which heirlooms—either physical objects or digital artifacts—would you like to pass down to your kids?

I'm extremely sentimental, and before she passed away in December 2019, my grandmother gave me some things that I’ll hold onto forever. My grandmother was from Guyana, where it’s very common for kids to wear bangles and I have the ones she wore when she was younger. And the first ring that my grandfather ever gave her, she passed down to me—I can't wait to pass it on to my girls. I'm really excited about my girls turning an age where they can understand and be grateful for things like that.

 

Other than that, my heirlooms are going to be photos and videos. My children don't just have a well-documented childhood—they have a beautifully documented childhood. My baby photos were from disposable cameras and although I'm so grateful for them, I love that I'm able to give my kids the gift of a beautifully documented upbringing.

How have you been keeping in touch with any faraway loved ones during the past year?

**Since most of my family and friends still live in Dallas, we’ve been seeing them in safe ways throughout COVID. But my husband’s family is in Houston, about a four-hour drive away, so we’ve FaceTimed a lot, especially with the kids. Even in my wildest imagination, I couldn’t have pictured what 2020 would have looked like. But having all this technology has been a wonderful way to stay close.**
